# Task 3 — Causality Assessment

## Objective
Assess whether the suspected medicine has a reasonable relationship with the reported reaction.

## Case: Amoxicillin → rash/pruritus

### WHO-UMC-style assessment

| Criterion | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Time relationship | Reaction occurred after exposure | Supports causality |
| Alternative explanations | Not adequately documented | Follow-up required |
| Dechallenge | Improvement after discontinuation | Supports causality |
| Rechallenge | Not performed | No additional evidence |
| Pharmacological/clinical plausibility | Skin reactions are clinically plausible with beta-lactam exposure | Supports, but does not prove, causality |
| Objective evidence | Limited in the supplied case | Weakens certainty |

### Provisional conclusion
**WHO-UMC category: Probable/Likely (provisional).**

Rationale: A reasonable temporal relationship and improvement after withdrawal are supportive, while the absence of complete information about alternative causes and objective clinical findings prevents classification as Certain.

### Rechallenge
Not performed. Rechallenge should not be treated as required evidence and should never be deliberately induced merely to prove causality.

## Optional Naranjo demonstration
If the training platform requires a Naranjo score, document the answer to each question transparently and state which answers are unknown. Do not force a score from missing data.

A practical assessment should record:
- Previous conclusive reports?
- Event after drug administered?
- Improvement on withdrawal?
- Event on re-administration?
- Alternative causes?
- Objective evidence?
- Drug levels/dose-response where relevant?

### Important distinction
Causality assessment asks **“How likely is the medicine related to the event?”** It does not by itself determine whether the case is serious.

## Evidence
WHO-UMC explains that most spontaneous reports fall between the extremes of certain and unlikely, with probable/likely and possible being common categories. PvPI follows the WHO-UMC causality assessment approach.
